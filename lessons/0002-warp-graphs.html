Graphs of Graphs

Lesson 1 said: store transformations. Of what? This lesson gives you the thing AIΩN transforms, the WARP graph, and a picture of it you can draw from memory.

The problem it solves

Paper I opens: “Many computational systems are best described as graphs of graphs of graphs.” A program is a graph of functions calling each other. Each function is itself a tree of syntax. Each call might carry its own little record of how it was optimised. Normally these layers are glued together with “a pile of pointers and conventions” (Paper I, §1). A WARP makes the whole stack one object.

The definition, in two rules

A WARP (Worldline Algebra for Recursive Provenance) is either:

1. An atom. A piece of raw data we don't look inside: a number, a string, an ID.

2. A skeleton with attachments. An ordinary graph (dots and arrows), where every dot and every arrow carries another WARP.

That's it (Paper I, Definition 1). Rule 2 refers back to “WARP”, so the nesting can go as deep as you like, but always bottoms out in atoms after finitely many steps. The paper calls this well-founded: no infinite nesting, no loops of containment.

The key word is every arrow. Many “nested graph” schemes only let nodes contain things. In a WARP, edges carry full WARPs too.

On a whiteboard: draw a big box with two dots and an arrow. Then draw a smaller box hanging off each dot and the arrow. Put a mini-graph in each. That's the drawing you'll be asked for.

Depth

The depth of a WARP is how many boxes deep you can go. Atoms have depth 0. A skeleton is one more than its deepest attachment (Paper I, the “Depth” definition). Above: the attachments are skeletons of atoms (depth 1), so the whole thing has depth 2.

Say it out loud

Explain a WARP to an imaginary engineer in under 45 seconds, without the words “category”, “algebra” or “recursive”. Use an example that isn't a call graph. Then draw the picture on paper from memory.

Model answer

“It's a graph where everything in it, every node and every link, can contain another graph, which can contain another, until you hit plain data. Think of a city map: each building contains a floor plan, each road carries its own traffic-history graph. Instead of a map plus a folder of separate files linked by IDs, it's one object. That one object is what AIΩN records changes to.”
